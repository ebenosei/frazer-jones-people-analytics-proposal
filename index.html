03 · Proactive Piece

A People Analytics Playbook for industrial workforces, 2026 edition.

Your client's industry (engineering, manufacturing, industrial) is in the middle of the steepest workforce transition since automation arrived in the 1970s. Below: three challenges I would expect to land on the People Analytics roadmap inside the first six months, each paired with the analytics product I would scope, build, and measure against.

Sector: Engineering · Manufacturing · Industrial Horizon: 2026 – 2028 Source-anchored
01
2.4M unfilled US manufacturing jobs by 2028 · Deloitte

The skills-gap problem is no longer a headcount problem.

Industrial employers are not short of people; they are short of the specific capabilities demanded by automation, robotics, and AI-augmented production. Of the ~3.8M net new manufacturing roles projected through 2033, roughly half (1.9M) are at risk of going unfilled because the supply side cannot retool fast enough. Workforce planning has to shift from headcount forecasting to skills inventory and skills supply modeling.

What I would build in the first 6 months

A Skills Supply & Gap Model

A unified skills taxonomy mapped against current workforce capability (from HRIS, LMS, and certifications), then projected against the skill demand implied by the business's automation and capex roadmap.

  • Data layer: Workday/SAP/Oracle skills records + LMS completions + certifications + open-source taxonomies (O*NET, ESCO) reconciled into a single ontology.
  • Modeling: Markov-chain transition modeling for internal mobility; logistic regression for "build vs. buy vs. partner" classification at the skill level.
  • Output: An executive dashboard answering three questions monthly: where are we capability-short, what's the cheapest closure path, and what's the time-to-close confidence interval?
  • KPI ladder: % of critical roles with internal successor in 12 months · time-to-close for top-10 skill gaps · cost per skill closed (build vs. hire vs. contract).
02
$16.7B manufacturing AI investment by 2026 · Industry analysts

Predictive attrition has to get sector-specific to be useful.

Generic attrition models (tenure, engagement, manager rating) collapse on industrial workforces because the most expensive departures are concentrated in narrow technical populations: skilled trades, plant engineers, EHS specialists, controls technicians. Losing one is operationally disruptive in a way that losing a headcount line in the model cannot capture. The model needs to be weighted by operational impact, not by headcount.

What I would build

Operationally-Weighted Attrition Prediction

An evolution of the model I built at mPharma (scikit-learn + XGBoost + SHAP for explainability), tuned to industrial workforce dynamics. Three deltas from the generic version:

  • Impact-weighted loss function: Predictions are scored against an "operational criticality index" combining replacement difficulty, internal successor depth, line/site exposure, and revenue-at-risk, not raw turnover probability.
  • Shift-pattern & safety features: Schedule volatility, overtime saturation, near-miss incident exposure, and physical-environment factors enter the feature space; these matter in industrial settings in a way they don't in white-collar populations.
  • SHAP-explained manager routing: Each at-risk prediction goes to the manager with the top three driver explanations and a vetted intervention playbook. No black-box outputs leave my team. This is what made the mPharma rollout actually move turnover instead of just predicting it.
03
Aging workforce: industrial demographics skew older than US average · BLS/Deloitte

Workforce planning belongs in the same room as Finance and Operations, not next to them.

An industrial workforce with replacement demand rising, capital projects with multi-year horizons, and Finance planning headcount on annual budgets cannot stay aligned with three separate planning cycles. The People Analytics function has to provide the shared, decision-grade dataset. This is the part of the JD asking for "bridging HR, Finance, IT, and business operations," and it's where most People Analytics teams under-deliver because they stay in the HR planning cycle.

What I would build

Integrated Workforce Planning Layer

A single workforce planning data product co-owned with FP&A and operations leadership. One source of truth, three lenses (HR / Finance / Ops), updated on the same cadence.

  • Scenario modeling: "What happens to our skills inventory and labor cost if we automate Line 4 in Q3?" Answered in minutes, not weeks.
  • Retirement-risk forecast: Age-tenure-role demographic modeling with confidence intervals on retirement timing per critical role family.
  • Governance: Definition standards co-signed by HR, Finance, and BI. One headcount definition, one FTE definition, one attrition definition. This is unglamorous and the entire function depends on it.
  • Adoption: Quarterly executive readouts and a self-service tier for HRBPs. The dashboard does not exist unless it's being used; that's a KPI I would put on my own team.

Sources: Deloitte "Skills, applicant gaps threaten US manufacturing growth" · i4cp "4 Priorities for People Analytics Leaders in 2026" · Deloitte/MI "Taking charge: Manufacturers support growth with active workforce strategies" · BLS workforce demographics.

04 · First 90 Days

How I would actually start.

A plan I would refine in week one after meeting the team, but this is how I think about ramping into an analytics leadership role. The pattern is the same one that worked at mPharma: listen before you build, build something small that proves the function, then earn the roadmap.

Days 0–30 · Listen & baseline

Find the truth in the data.

  • 1:1s with HR centers of expertise, Finance partners, and 3–5 line-of-business leaders. Capture insight needs verbatim.
  • Audit current dashboards, reports, and data sources. Document definitions, owners, refresh cadence, and trust scores.
  • Inventory analytics requests in the queue and classify (reporting / insight / model / governance).
  • Identify the three datasets that, if cleaned and trusted, would unlock the next eighteen months of work.
Days 31–60 · Prove the function

Ship one thing that matters.

  • Select one high-impact, scope-limited analytics product (most likely an executive workforce dashboard with credible definitions and clear narrative) and ship it with the CHRO and CFO in the room.
  • Publish the data definitions library (v0.1). Co-sign with Finance, IT, and HR ops.
  • Stand up the People Analytics roadmap as a living document with prioritization criteria visible to stakeholders.
  • Run one "translate complex into clear" executive readout. Set the tone for how this team communicates.
Days 61–90 · Earn the roadmap

From dashboard to decision system.

  • Scope the first predictive product (operationally-weighted attrition or skills supply, depending on what the diagnostic surfaces) with a defined business owner and adoption KPI.
  • Codify data governance: HR data quality standards, privacy and ethical guidelines, alignment with enterprise BI architecture.
  • Submit Q3+Q4 roadmap and team-shape proposal to HR leadership.
  • Build the rhythm: monthly insight readouts, quarterly stakeholder steering, ongoing self-serve enablement.
